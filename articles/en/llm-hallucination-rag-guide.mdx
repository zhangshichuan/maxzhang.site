What shocks most people the first time they use a large language model is usually not what it gets right, but how confident it can sound when it is wrong.

You ask a specific question, and the model may:

- fabricate data that looks plausible
- invent a paper or book that does not exist
- quietly rewrite your request into a version that is easier to answer
- produce a polished, detailed conclusion with no real basis

This is one of the most classic and frustrating problems in LLM development:

**hallucination.**

What makes hallucination dangerous is not just that the answer is wrong. It is that the answer is often wrong in a way that looks right.
If a system occasionally stays silent, users can often tolerate it. But if it confidently makes things up at a high frequency, the trust foundation of the whole system collapses quickly.

So the goal of this article is straightforward:

**to explain LLM hallucination from definition to root causes to practical mitigation strategies.**

We will go through:

- what factual hallucination is versus faithfulness hallucination
- why hallucinations happen in the first place
- what goes wrong at the data, model, and architecture layers
- why exposure bias, capability mismatch, and belief mismatch show up
- why models often over-focus on nearby text
- what knowledge editing, retrieval augmentation, and RAG each solve
- what vector databases, embeddings, and similarity search are doing inside RAG
- how LangChain Documents and LangGraph fit into a real engineering workflow

---

# What hallucination actually is

Put simply, hallucination means:

**the model generates content that sounds reasonable but is either untrue or not faithful to the input, evidence, or facts.**

Many people think hallucination is just one thing. In practice, you need to split it into at least two categories, otherwise the discussion becomes muddled very quickly.

---

## 1. Factual hallucination: saying things that are not true

Factual hallucination means the model outputs content that does not match objective reality.

For example:

- inventing a book, paper, or author that does not exist
- stating the wrong year, number, or company fact as if it were true
- giving outdated answers to real-time questions
- producing incorrect conclusions in technical or professional domains

For example, suppose you ask:

> In what year did OpenAI release a certain product?

If the model gives a year and that year is simply wrong in the real world, that is a typical factual hallucination.

Its core issue is:

**the output does not match the actual external world.**

---

## 2. Faithfulness hallucination: failing to stay faithful to the input, context, or evidence

Faithfulness hallucination is easier to overlook.

The output may not always be wrong in the objective world, but it is not faithful to the given input, context, document evidence, or instruction constraints.

For example:

- you explicitly ask it to answer only based on provided material, and it still improvises
- the user says "if you do not know, say so," but it answers anyway
- the source document says A, but the model summarizes it as B
- you ask for extraction, but it adds its own inference

Imagine you provide a contract and ask for a summary of the breach clauses.
The original text contains only two clauses, but the model adds a third "common clause" from its own knowledge. It may not be making up nonsense in a general sense, but it is no longer faithful to the evidence you gave it.

So the core issue in faithfulness hallucination is not "is it objectively correct," but:

**did the output honestly stay within the boundary it was supposed to stay within?**

---

# Why it matters to separate these two types

Because they are not mitigated in exactly the same way.

If the problem is factual hallucination, you are more likely to think about:

- external knowledge verification
- retrieval augmentation
- access to up-to-date data
- knowledge updates

If the problem is faithfulness hallucination, you are more likely to think about:

- prompt constraints
- evidence citation
- output format restrictions
- post-hoc verification
- workflow decomposition

Many teams say, "We already added RAG, so why is the system still unstable?"
Often the reason is that they only looked at factual hallucination and failed to realize that many of the remaining issues are really faithfulness hallucinations.

# Why hallucinations happen

To understand mitigation, you first have to accept a basic fact:

**large language models were not originally designed to work by checking facts first and answering second.**

Their core training objective is next-token prediction.
At the lowest level, what they are best at is not "pursuing truth," but:

**generating the most likely continuation under the current context.**

That sentence matters a lot.

Because it means the model is naturally optimizing for:

- linguistic coherence
- statistical likelihood
- smooth local continuation

not for:

- truth in the real world
- rigorous multi-step reasoning
- verifiable loyalty to external facts

That is why hallucination is not some rare bug. It is a structural gap between the training objective of the model and the reliability objective of real applications.

---

# The roots of hallucination: data layer, model layer, and architecture layer

If you go one level deeper, the sources of hallucination can be split into at least three layers.

---

## 1. The data layer: the training data was never perfect to begin with

Many people assume that since LLMs are trained on massive internet-scale data, they should just know everything.
But the real problem is this:

**more data does not mean truer data.**

At the data layer, several problems are common.

## 1. The data already contains errors

Internet text is full of:

- outdated information
- pseudo-knowledge
- incorrect retellings from media or blogs
- forum speculation
- unverified second-hand content

If the model absorbs all of this at scale, it naturally learns some of the wrong patterns too.

## 2. The data distribution is uneven

Some topics are overrepresented, while many niche professional domains are underrepresented.
As a result, the model may look very strong on mainstream topics but start making things up in long-tail domains.

## 3. The data has limited freshness

Pretraining is effectively a snapshot of the world frozen before a certain point in time.
So once you ask about:

- the latest policy
- the latest software version
- the latest price
- the latest personnel change

the model is highly likely to repeat stale information unless it has access to external retrieval.

## 4. Human language itself is ambiguous

Human language is full of omission, implication, exaggeration, and ambiguity.
What the model learns is that linguistic distribution, not a clean and structured knowledge base.

So from the beginning, the "world" the model sees is not a precise, clean, verifiable truth repository. It is a noisy ocean of text filled with bias, contradiction, outdated content, and partial information.

---

## 2. The model layer: being able to speak does not mean being able to judge truth

Even if the data were good enough, the model layer still has natural limitations.

---

## 1. Architectural mismatch: language modeling is not the same as truth modeling

Transformers are powerful, but what they fundamentally solve is sequence modeling.
Their training objective is:

- given prior context
- predict the next token

That is enough to produce astonishing generative ability, but it does not directly require the model to be accountable to facts.

In other words:

**the model learns what sounds like something a person would say, not what must be true.**

That is the deepest architectural mismatch.

---

## 2. Exposure bias: during training the model sees ground-truth context, during inference it sees its own outputs

Exposure bias is a common issue in generative models.

During training, each step usually sees the correct context.
But during inference, later tokens depend on text the model itself has already generated.

What does that imply?

If one step drifts, the following steps continue on top of that drift.

That is why you often see a typical pattern:

- the beginning looks normal
- the middle starts drifting quietly
- the later part sounds smoother and smoother while becoming more and more wrong

This is not because the model suddenly "goes crazy." It is because once the generation process enters a bad trajectory, later tokens still keep optimizing for local coherence.

---

## 3. Capability mismatch: the user wants facts, the model is best at continuation

Many real tasks require:

- retrieval
- comparison
- citation
- logical checking
- multi-step decision making

But those are not the model's strongest native skills.
What it is especially good at is:

- generating language from context
- filling in missing detail probabilistically
- keeping the response smooth even under uncertainty

So you get a capability mismatch:

**users treat the model as a QA system, search engine, expert system, or decision system, while underneath it is still closer to an extremely strong probabilistic generator.**

Once that mismatch appears, hallucination becomes almost inevitable.

---

## 4. Belief mismatch: sacrificing truthfulness in order to be cooperative

This is especially visible in chat-oriented models.

Many models shaped by instruction tuning and RLHF are strongly optimized to be:

- more willing to answer
- more cooperative
- less likely to refuse
- more likely to "understand what you mean"

That improves interaction quality, but it also introduces another problem:

**the model can start optimizing for helpfulness and compliance at the expense of truthfulness.**

Typical symptoms include:

- the user hints at a conclusion and the model follows along
- the model gives a full answer even when there is not enough evidence
- it should say "I am not sure," but it keeps expanding because that sounds more helpful

That is a kind of belief mismatch:

the model has been optimized to act more like a cooperative partner, but part of factual reliability gets traded away in the process.

---

## 5. Over-attending to nearby text: local smoothness beats global correctness

During generation, LLMs naturally care a lot about nearby context.
That is one reason they are powerful, but it is also one reason they hallucinate.

Because in many cases, "what flows best locally" is not the same as "what is globally correct."

For example:

- one sentence mentions a similar concept, so the next sentence casually continues with it
- the text sounds academic, so the model keeps generating academic-sounding prose
- the document provides only half of the clues, and the model automatically fills in the rest

That is why the model can often produce text that is:

- stylistically convincing
- structurally convincing
- terminologically convincing
- factually wrong in the details

It is optimizing for continuity in the local text distribution, not necessarily for global factual verification.

---

# So how do we mitigate hallucinations?

Start with a realistic conclusion:

**hallucination is almost impossible to eliminate completely, but it can be reduced substantially and constrained through engineering.**

Broadly speaking, mitigation strategies fall into three groups:

1. directly modify the knowledge inside the model
2. supplement the model with external knowledge
3. put the model's outputs into a stricter workflow with verification

Let us go through them one by one.

---

## 1. Knowledge editing: directly changing what the model "knows"

Knowledge editing means:

**modifying certain pieces of internal model knowledge without fully retraining the model from scratch.**

For example:

- updating an outdated fact
- correcting a specific false memory
- strengthening local knowledge in a certain domain

This is most suitable when:

- a stable fact is persistently wrong
- some domain-specific knowledge must be force-updated
- you do not want to rely on external retrieval every time

But knowledge editing has clear drawbacks:

- it is costly
- it is difficult
- it can affect neighboring knowledge unexpectedly
- it is good for targeted fixes, not for highly dynamic information

So it is not usually the first practical choice for everyday application systems.
In real product engineering, retrieval augmentation is much more common.

---

## 2. Retrieval augmentation: do not let the model answer from memory alone

The core idea of retrieval augmentation is actually very simple:

**if the model does not know, do not force it to guess from parametric memory; retrieve evidence first, then answer.**

This is also the core idea behind RAG.

Retrieval augmentation can happen at different moments in the pipeline.

---

## 1. One-shot retrieval

This is the most common setup.

The flow usually looks like:

1. the user asks a question
2. the system retrieves relevant material
3. the retrieved context is sent to the model together with the question
4. the model answers based on that context

Its advantages are:

- simple
- cheap
- fast

Its weaknesses are:

- it retrieves only once, so it may miss crucial information
- if the original user question is poorly phrased, the retrieval may also go in the wrong direction

---

## 2. Iterative retrieval

Some questions are too complex for one retrieval step.
The user query may be vague, or the problem may require multi-hop reasoning.

In that case, the system may:

1. retrieve once
2. let the model identify information gaps
3. generate a follow-up retrieval query
4. retrieve again
5. produce the final answer after gathering enough evidence

This is closer to:

**thinking while searching, instead of searching only once.**

It fits:

- multi-hop questions
- graph-like knowledge tasks
- tasks where the original question is underspecified and has to be clarified progressively

But it also costs more:

- higher latency
- more workflow complexity
- stronger dependence on orchestration and state management

---

## 3. Post-hoc retrieval

There is also another approach: not "retrieve first, answer second," but "answer first, verify second."

For example:

1. the model produces a first draft
2. the system extracts key factual claims
3. it retrieves evidence to verify those claims
4. if conflicts are found, the model is asked to revise

This is especially useful for:

- high-risk answers
- citation-heavy tasks
- report generation
- long-form summarization

Because in some applications, what matters most is:

**whether the final answer is verifiable, not whether the model got it right in one shot.**

---

# What RAG is, and why it matters so much

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation.

In one sentence:

**RAG means retrieving evidence first, then letting the model generate an answer grounded in that evidence.**

Why does it matter so much?
Because it sits at a very practical balance point.

---

## The core value of RAG

## 1. It is economical

If you just want the model to know a set of private knowledge, fully retraining or even fine-tuning may be expensive.
RAG usually only requires:

- building a knowledge base
- building an index
- performing retrieval
- injecting relevant passages into the model context

That is much cheaper and much more flexible than retraining a model.

---

## 2. It keeps information fresh

Knowledge stored in model parameters is not real-time.
An external knowledge base can be.

For example:

- the latest product documentation
- internal process manuals
- recent support tickets
- newly published API specifications

As long as the knowledge base is updated, the RAG system can use newer information.

---

## 3. It improves user trust

If a system not only answers, but also shows:

- source passages
- cited documents
- reference links

users are more likely to trust it.

In many scenarios, users do not just want "an answer that sounds right." They want to know:

**what exactly that answer is based on.**

That traceability is one of the biggest strengths of RAG.

---

## 4. It gives developers more control

With pure parametric memory, developers have limited control over exactly which knowledge the model is using.
With a RAG pipeline, developers can control:

- which documents are included
- how documents are chunked
- how retrieval is done
- how many chunks are retrieved
- how reranking works
- how citation is handled

In other words, RAG takes back part of the knowledge-control problem from the model's black box.

---

# Why RAG depends so much on vector databases

To understand vector databases, it helps not to start with the abstract definition.
Start with a simpler analogy.

Suppose you want to describe different cats. You might use features such as:

- body size
- fur
- leg length
- sound
- ears

If each feature gets mapped to a number, then each cat can be represented as a list of numbers.
If two cats have overall similar numbers, they are more similar.

That is the intuition behind vectorization.

Text works in a similar way.
A sentence, a paragraph, or a document title can be mapped into a high-dimensional array of numbers.
The more similar the meaning, the closer they often are in vector space.

That is also why:

- face recognition can compare likeness
- image search can find similar images
- text retrieval can find semantically close content

because under the hood, they are not just matching exact symbols. They are comparing vectors.

---

## How vector databases differ from traditional databases

This is not about one being universally better than the other. They solve different problems.

---

## 1. Search difference

Traditional databases are excellent at exact matching, such as:

- `id = 1001`
- `status = paid`
- `name like '%phone%'`

They solve field-based matching.

But semantic retrieval wants to answer questions like:

- Is this passage semantically close to the user query?
- Is this document chunk talking about the same concept?
- Is the vector for this chunk close to the query vector?

At that point, keyword matching alone is not enough. You need similarity search.

---

## 2. Storage difference

Traditional databases mainly store:

- tables
- rows
- columns
- indexes

Vector databases also store raw text and metadata, but they are especially optimized around:

- embedding vectors
- vector indexes
- data structures needed for similarity search

So a vector database is not just a rebranded traditional database. It is a storage system optimized for large-scale high-dimensional nearest-neighbor retrieval.

---

# How similarity search works

Once both the query and the documents are turned into vectors, the next step is comparing how similar they are.

Two common approaches are:

---

## 1. Cosine similarity

Cosine similarity measures how close the angle is between two vectors.

It mainly cares about:

**whether the directions are similar.**

This is very common in text applications, because semantic similarity often cares more about directional similarity than about absolute magnitude.

That is why cosine similarity is especially suitable for:

- text vectors
- semantic image vectors
- high-dimensional semantic retrieval

---

## 2. Euclidean distance

Euclidean distance measures the straight-line distance between two vectors.

It cares more about:

**absolute geometric distance.**

It is still useful in some tasks, but in high-dimensional semantic text spaces, many systems prefer cosine similarity or related variants.

---

# Why similarity search needs acceleration

At first glance, retrieval sounds simple: compare the query vector to every document vector and pick the nearest ones.

Theoretically, yes.
But once the knowledge base becomes large, this gets very expensive.

Because you may be comparing against not hundreds of vectors, but:

- hundreds of thousands
- millions
- hundreds of millions

So in practice, systems rely on approximate nearest neighbor methods and vector index structures to accelerate retrieval.

That is why vector databases matter not just because they can store vectors, but because they can:

**efficiently find near neighbors in very large vector collections.**

---

# What embedding models are

An embedding model maps unstructured data into meaningful vectors.

That data can include:

- text
- images
- audio
- video

In RAG, the most common case is text embeddings.

For example:

- a user question
- a document passage
- an FAQ entry
- a chunk from a knowledge base

can all be turned into vectors by an embedding model and then compared through similarity search.

---

## Classical embedding models

In the history of embeddings, some classic names include:

- `word2vec`
- `GloVe`
- `fastText`

These belong more to the earlier era of word-level embeddings.
Modern RAG usually relies on sentence-level, paragraph-level, or document-level embedding models instead.

Still, those earlier methods are useful to understand because they reveal the core value of vector representations.

---

## Why embeddings matter

## 1. Dimensional reduction

They turn complex unstructured content into a numerical form that can be compared and computed on.

## 2. Semantic capture

They let the system compare meaning, not just exact surface words.

## 3. Adaptability

The same retrieval framework can be used across different text types and tasks.

## 4. Generalization

Even if a query and a document do not share exactly the same words, they can still be matched.

## 5. Partial interpretability

The vector space itself is not fully human-readable, but similarity results still give you a way to understand which pieces of content the system sees as semantically close.

---

# Why vector operations matter

Vectors are not useful only because they can be stored. They are useful because they can be computed on.

In semantic space, common operations include:

- comparing vector similarity
- finding nearest neighbors
- clustering
- classification
- generating reranking features

These operations make semantics computable.
That is one of the most important foundations behind modern RAG, recommendation systems, and semantic search.

---

# How a typical RAG system works

If you unfold the pipeline, a typical RAG system often includes these steps:

1. load raw documents
2. clean the documents
3. chunk the documents
4. generate embeddings for each chunk
5. store vectors and metadata in a vector database
6. generate an embedding for the user query
7. retrieve relevant chunks through vector search
8. rerank if needed
9. send retrieved context to the LLM
10. ask the model to answer based on the evidence

Every one of these steps can affect the final hallucination rate.

Many people assume that RAG success or failure depends mostly on the model. That is not really true.
RAG often succeeds or fails more because of:

- how you chunk
- how you retrieve
- how you rerank
- how you feed evidence into the model
- how you constrain the model's answer

# What LangChain's Document component does here

The moment you build RAG, you quickly realize that raw knowledge is not naturally ready to be fed into the model.

Documents may come from:

- PDFs
- web pages
- Markdown
- Notion
- databases
- internal knowledge systems

These formats are mixed, and they often come with useful metadata.

LangChain's `Document` abstraction exists to represent a unit of knowledge in a uniform structure.

A `Document` usually has at least two parts:

- `page_content`: the main content
- `metadata`: additional information

For example:

```ts
const doc = {
  pageContent: 'RAG can reduce hallucinations by grounding generation in retrieved documents.',
  metadata: {
    source: 'whitepaper.pdf',
    page: 12,
    section: 'retrieval',
  },
}
```

Its value is:

- content from different sources can be processed uniformly
- chunked pieces still preserve source information
- retrieval results can return together with provenance
- citation, filtering, and permission control become easier downstream

Many higher-trust RAG systems look more credible not because they "speak better," but because they preserve source traceability better.

# RAG optimization strategies

RAG does not mean "throw documents into a vector store and you are done."
Effective RAG usually depends on multiple layers of optimization.

---

## 1. Improve chunking strategy

If chunks are too large:

- retrieval becomes noisy
- context windows waste tokens

If chunks are too small:

- meaning becomes incomplete
- crucial context gets split apart

So chunking should consider things like:

- fixed length versus semantic segmentation
- whether to keep overlap
- whether to split by heading hierarchy
- whether to preserve tables, sections, or FAQ structures

Chunking strategy is often more important than switching to a stronger model.

---

## 2. Improve the query

User questions are not always good retrieval queries by default.

For example, suppose the user asks:

> Why does this API keep failing?

For retrieval, that query is too vague.
A stronger system might first rewrite it into something involving:

- the interface name
- the error code
- the relevant module
- the time range

That is query rewriting or query expansion.

---

## 3. Hybrid retrieval

Pure vector retrieval is not always best.

Some tasks work better with:

- sparse retrieval like BM25
- dense retrieval with embeddings
- a hybrid of both

Because:

- BM25 is strong when exact keywords matter
- vector retrieval is strong when semantic equivalence matters

Hybrid retrieval is often more stable.

---

## 4. Reranking

The first retrieval stage does not always produce the best final ordering.

That is why many systems add a reranking stage after retrieval, using a more expensive but more accurate model to reorder candidates.

This matters a lot because:

**generation quality often fails not because nothing relevant was retrieved, but because the most relevant evidence was not ranked near the top.**

---

## 5. Strengthen citation and answer constraints

To reduce faithfulness hallucination, the generation stage can explicitly require:

- answer only from retrieved evidence
- say "I do not know" when evidence is insufficient
- cite the corresponding source
- separate quoted evidence from model inference

These constraints do not eliminate hallucination, but they can significantly reduce the model's tendency to freely elaborate beyond evidence.

---

## 6. Add post-hoc verification

For high-value outputs, add a verification layer:

- extract key factual claims
- check whether those claims are supported by evidence
- verify that citations actually exist
- detect expansions unsupported by the retrieved context

If the answer fails validation, send it into a revision path.

---

## 7. Route by task type

Not every task should go through the same RAG pipeline.

For example:

- FAQ search
- contract Q&A
- codebase retrieval
- report generation

These have very different data structures, retrieval patterns, and answer formats.
Trying to push them all through one shared pipeline usually makes the system worse over time.

A better approach is task-based routing with specialized optimization for each path.

---

# RAG is not a silver bullet

At this point it is worth being explicit:

RAG is important, but it does not automatically solve every hallucination problem.

There are at least a few reasons why.

## 1. If retrieval is wrong, generation will still be wrong

If the system retrieves incorrect evidence, the model will simply answer more confidently from incorrect evidence.

## 2. Even when retrieval succeeds, the model may still use the evidence unfaithfully

This is exactly where faithfulness hallucination shows up.
Bringing evidence into context does not guarantee that the model will stay loyal to it.

## 3. If the context is too long, the key evidence may be drowned out

If you stuff in too many passages, the truly relevant ones may become less salient.

## 4. Some problems are not really retrieval-only problems

For example:

- complex planning
- multi-step decisions
- tool-use chains
- stateful interaction flows

At that point, you need a higher-level workflow layer.

That is where LangGraph becomes important.

---

# Why LangGraph matters

Many people eventually hit the same limitation with basic LangChain pipelines:

simple chains are fine, but once the workflow becomes more complex, a plain linear chain is not enough.

For example, a more complete anti-hallucination workflow might look like:

1. the user asks a question
2. the system decides whether retrieval is needed
3. it retrieves
4. it evaluates whether the evidence is sufficient
5. if not, it launches another retrieval round
6. it generates a draft answer
7. it performs factual verification
8. if verification fails, it routes back to revision
9. it returns the final answer

This is no longer a simple sequential pipeline.
It is a graph-shaped workflow with branches, loops, and state.

That is where LangGraph becomes useful:

**it upgrades an LLM application from sequential calling into a controllable workflow.**

For hallucination mitigation, it is especially well suited to:

- iterative retrieval
- retrieval followed by verification
- multi-stage generation
- self-reflection
- fallback logic
- inserting human review into the loop

If RAG is about "feeding the model better evidence," then LangGraph is more like:

**building a workflow that makes it harder for the model to go off the rails.**

# A more complete engineering strategy for reducing hallucination

If we compress everything above into a more practical engineering playbook, it looks something like this.

---

## Layer 1: identify which kind of hallucination you are dealing with

Do not just say "the model hallucinated."
First determine:

- is it factual hallucination?
- or is it faithfulness hallucination?

Because the mitigation priorities differ.

---

## Layer 2: when you do not have to rely on parametric memory, do not

If the task depends heavily on external knowledge, prefer:

- retrieval augmentation
- citation mechanisms
- tool use
- live data sources

instead of assuming the model "should already know."

---

## Layer 3: turn knowledge into retrievable objects

What determines RAG success is not just "having a knowledge base," but:

- whether the document structure is clean
- whether the metadata is complete
- whether chunking is sensible
- whether the embedding model is appropriate
- whether retrieval and reranking match the task

This is knowledge engineering, not just model engineering.

---

## Layer 4: put generation inside a controlled workflow

Do not place all your hope in a single well-written prompt.

More reliable systems usually do some version of:

- retrieve
- generate
- verify
- retry or revise when needed

That is the real point of graph-based workflows.

---

## Layer 5: make "I cannot tell" a system capability, not a failure mode

Many hallucinations come from a very basic product mistake:

**the system is designed as if it must always answer.**

But a higher-quality system should be allowed to:

- say it does not know
- say the evidence is insufficient
- ask for more information
- say that it cannot verify the claim yet

If the product layer insists that the system must always provide a complete answer, the model will keep hallucinating in order to satisfy that requirement.

---

# Summary

Hallucination is hard not because the model occasionally gets something wrong, but because the model was never structurally designed to be a truth machine.

When hallucination appears, the causes usually involve several layers at once:

- noise, staleness, and bias in the training data
- a generative objective that is misaligned with factual reliability
- architecture that lacks retrieval, verification, and constraint
- interaction tuning that favors cooperation and local continuation over global correctness

That is why the most useful mitigation strategy is never just "find a smarter model."
Real systems usually need a stack like this:

- knowledge editing to patch a few critical facts
- retrieval augmentation to reduce unsupported guessing
- RAG to inject external knowledge into the answer process
- vector databases and embeddings to enable semantic retrieval
- LangChain Documents to represent knowledge in a uniform way
- RAG optimization strategies to improve recall and faithfulness
- LangGraph to put answering inside a controllable workflow

For real AI applications, the real goal is not "the model never makes mistakes."
It is this:

**when the model knows, help it answer correctly; when it does not know, stop it from pretending; and when it must answer, make the result traceable, verifiable, and correctable.**

That is one of the clearest dividing lines between an impressive demo and a production-grade LLM system.
